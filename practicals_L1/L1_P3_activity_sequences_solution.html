Lecture 1 · Practical 3 — When neural networks clearly win¶

Early warning of hospital admissions from daily step counts: CNN, RNN and LSTM against logistic regression and gradient boosting¶

Version: instructor solution

Where this notebook sits in Lecture 1¶

Practicals 1 and 2 showed tasks in which neural networks did not beat simpler models: the useful information could be summarised in a few columns that any model can use. This practical shows the opposite case, and explains why the networks win.

The business question. Arvena's supplementary outpatient product includes Move+, a step-bonus app: members who share their daily step counts earn small rewards. Arvena's care-management team can call a limited number of members each month and offer support (a nurse call, a check-up reminder). Which members will be admitted to hospital within the next 45 days? If the team can reach them earlier, some admissions may be avoided or shortened — and hospital stays are the largest single block of health costs (Practical 1).

The data are daily step counts of 2,638 participants from 2023 to 2026.

Learning goals¶

  1. Turn long time series into a supervised task without leakage, and evaluate a rare-event classifier with a business metric (admissions found in the 5% of members the team can call).
  2. Build a 1D convolutional network, a vanilla RNN and an LSTM, and compare them with logistic regression and gradient boosting on (a) summary statistics and (b) the raw days.
  3. Explain why the convolutional network wins, with a controlled experiment: inductive bias, weight sharing and translation equivariance.
  4. See the vanishing-gradient problem of vanilla RNNs in action, and why LSTMs fix it.

About the data. The step counts are synthetic and were generated from documented mechanisms (generate_activity.py): personal activity level, weekday pattern, seasons, holidays, days without the device, colds, accidents, hospital stays, and — before many admissions — warning signs. We describe them in Section 1 because the point of the practical is to see which model families can learn which structures. Real wearable data are messier, but published studies detect illness from deviations of wearable signals from a person's own baseline (Radin et al., 2020; Mishra et al., 2020).

Runtime: about 8 minutes on a laptop CPU (much faster on a GPU).

1.1 · What is in a series¶

Each participant's series combines the following mechanisms. Only the last one carries information about a coming admission.

mechanism what it looks like predictive?
personal level some people walk 3,000 steps a day, others 15,000 weakly (older, sicker people walk less)
weekday pattern, seasons weekly rhythm; more steps in summer no
holidays 1–3 weeks clearly higher or lower, abrupt start and end no
device not worn runs of zeros no
colds and short illnesses (about 3 per year) sharp drop for a few days, full recovery within a week no
accidents drop and recovery over days or weeks barely
warning signs before many admissions one to three flare-ups: a sharp drop for a few days, then a slow and incomplete recovery, at a time that varies from 3 weeks to several months before admission yes

A flare-up and a cold look alike on the day of the drop. They differ in the shape of the recovery — and a flare-up can sit anywhere in the observed window.

2 · The prediction task¶

  • Prediction dates: the first day of a month. For each date and each participant observed for the previous 182 days and the next 45 days, we create one example.
  • Input: the 182 daily step counts before the date, plus four facts known from the claims: age, sex, chronic medication last year, hospital stays last year.
  • Label: 1 if the participant is admitted to hospital within the next 45 days.
  • Split by time: train on prediction dates July 2023 – March 2025, validate on April – September 2025, test on 2026.

2.1 · Metrics for a rare event and a limited team¶

About 1.5% of windows end in an admission. Besides ROC-AUC and average precision (AP), we use the metric the care-management team cares about: the share of all upcoming admissions found among the 5% of members flagged as riskiest (recall at 5%), and the share of flagged members who are actually admitted (precision at 5%). A random list would find 5% of admissions.

# ✅ SOLUTION
def capture(y, p, share=0.05):
    k = int(round(share * len(p))); top = np.argsort(-p)[:k]
    return y[top].sum() / y.sum(), y[top].mean()

4 · Neural networks on the raw sequence¶

4.1 · Preparing a sequence for a network¶

  • log scale: step counts span 1,000 to 30,000; in logs, a 30% drop has the same size for everybody;
  • centring on the person's own level: we subtract each window's mean log steps on worn days, so the network sees deviations from the personal baseline (the level itself enters as an extra static input);
  • mask channel: a second channel is 1 on worn days and 0 otherwise, and the step channel is 0 on unworn days, so a missing day is not mistaken for a collapse in activity.

Each window becomes a tensor of shape (2 channels × 182 days).

In [8]:
def to_sequence(X):
    worn = (X > 0).astype(np.float32); L = np.log1p(X)
    level = (L * worn).sum(1, keepdims=True) / np.maximum(worn.sum(1, keepdims=True), 1)
    return torch.tensor(np.stack([np.where(worn > 0, L - level, 0), worn], 1), dtype=torch.float32), level
z_mu, z_sd = Ztr.mean(0), Ztr.std(0)
def to_static(Z, level): return torch.tensor(np.hstack([(Z - z_mu) / z_sd, level - 8.5]), dtype=torch.float32)
def tensors(X, Z, y):
    seq, level = to_sequence(X)
    return seq, to_static(Z, level), torch.tensor(y, dtype=torch.float32)
T_tr, T_va, T_te = tensors(Xtr, Ztr, ytr), tensors(Xva, Zva, yva), tensors(Xte, Zte, yte)
N_STATIC = T_tr[1].shape[1]
print("sequence tensor:", tuple(T_tr[0].shape), "· static inputs:", N_STATIC)

def train(model, epochs=15, lr=2e-3, batch=256, verbose=True):
    """AdamW, binary cross-entropy, keep the epoch with the best validation AUC"""
    torch.manual_seed(SEED); opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=lr, weight_decay=1e-3); best = (0, None, 0)
    for ep in range(epochs):
        t0 = time.time(); model.train(); perm = torch.randperm(len(T_tr[2]))
        for i in range(0, len(perm), batch):
            j = perm[i:i + batch]; opt.zero_grad()
            F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(model(T_tr[0][j], T_tr[1][j]), T_tr[2][j]).backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0); opt.step()
        model.eval()
        with torch.no_grad(): auc = roc_auc_score(yva, model(T_va[0], T_va[1]).numpy())
        if auc > best[0]: best = (auc, {k: v.clone() for k, v in model.state_dict().items()}, ep)
    model.load_state_dict(best[1]); model.eval()
    if verbose: print(f"best epoch {best[2] + 1}/{epochs} · validation AUC {best[0]:.3f} · {time.time() - t0:.1f} s per epoch")
    return model
def net_proba(model, X, Z):
    seq, level = to_sequence(X)
    with torch.no_grad(): return torch.sigmoid(model(seq, to_static(Z, level))).numpy()

4.2 · Baseline network: an MLP on the flattened days¶

The simplest network treats the 364 numbers as one long vector: every day has its own weights. It has the capacity to learn anything, but nothing in its structure says that day 100 and day 101 are neighbours.

4.3 · A 1D convolutional network¶

A convolution slides a small filter — here 7 days wide — along the series and computes, at every position $t$, a weighted sum of the days around $t$: $$ h_{c}(t) = \operatorname{ReLU}\Big(b_c + \sum_{k=-3}^{3}\ \sum_{\text{ch}} w_{c,\text{ch},k}\; x_{\text{ch}}(t+k)\Big). $$ Two properties matter:

  • weight sharing: the same filter is used at every position, so a pattern learned at day 40 is recognised at day 140 (translation equivariance);
  • hierarchy: stacking convolutions with pooling (keeping the maximum of neighbouring values) lets later layers see longer stretches: a drop, then what follows it.

Our network has three convolution layers (16, 32, 32 filters) and two max-pooling steps. At the end it averages each filter's response over the whole window (global average pooling: "did this pattern occur, and how strongly?") and over the last weeks ("is it happening now?"), and combines these summaries with the static inputs.

# ✅ SOLUTION
class ConvNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv1d(2, 16, 7, padding=3), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool1d(2),
                                  nn.Conv1d(16, 32, 7, padding=3), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool1d(2),
                                  nn.Conv1d(32, 32, 5, padding=2), nn.ReLU())
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 * 32 + N_STATIC, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 1))
    def forward(self, seq, static):
        h = self.conv(seq)                                   # (batch, 32 filters, 45 positions)
        return self.head(torch.cat([h.mean(2), h[:, :, -6:].mean(2), static], 1)).squeeze(1)

4.4 · Recurrent networks: vanilla RNN and LSTM¶

A recurrent network reads the 182 days one by one and updates a hidden state (Practical 1, Section 6.2). The final state must remember a flare-up that may have happened 100 days earlier.

  • In a vanilla RNN, the gradient that links the output to day $t$ is multiplied by the recurrent weight matrix (and a tanh derivative) once per day in between. Over a hundred steps it typically shrinks towards zero — the vanishing-gradient problem (Bengio et al., 1994) — so the network learns mostly from the last days.
  • An LSTM carries a cell state that is updated additively and controlled by gates; the forget gate can keep information unchanged for many steps (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997).

class RecurrentNet(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, cell):
        super().__init__()
        self.rnn = {"rnn": nn.RNN, "lstm": nn.LSTM}[cell](2, 32, batch_first=True)
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(32 + N_STATIC, 32), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(32, 1))
    def forward(self, seq, static):
        out, _ = self.rnn(seq.transpose(1, 2))         # (batch, days, channels) → hidden state after every day
        return self.head(torch.cat([out[:, -1], static], 1)).squeeze(1)

Reading the scoreboard.

  • The four facts from the claims alone (age, sex, chronic medication, past stays) already find some admissions: older and sicker people are admitted more often.
  • Activity data carry information for every model: summary statistics or the raw days raise the share of admissions found among the 5% highest scores from 23% to 38–39%.
  • The convolutional network and the LSTM find clearly more admissions with the same calling budget (47% and 51% against at most 39%) and roughly double the average precision (0.20 against 0.11 for the best model outside the network family). For a care team that can make a fixed number of calls, this means substantially more of the people who need help.
  • The flattened MLP and the vanilla RNN do not share this advantage: being a neural network is not enough. What matters is an architecture whose inductive bias — its built-in assumptions — matches the structure of the signal.

6 · Why do the CNN and the LSTM win? A controlled experiment¶

We take 400 test windows of people who were not admitted, and we implant an artificial event at a chosen day:

  • a flare-up: activity drops to 35% for 4 days, then recovers slowly over 18 days to only 90% of the previous level;
  • a cold: the same drop, then full recovery within 5 days.

We implant each event at day 30, 80 or 130 of the 182-day window and record how much each model raises its predicted risk (ratio of new to old prediction, averaged over the 400 people). A model that has learned the right pattern should react strongly to flare-ups, weakly to colds, and in the same way wherever the event sits.

        rows.append({"event": kind, "implanted at day": day, **{k: np.mean(f(X1) / base[k]) for k, f in predictors.items()}})

Reading the experiment (values are factors on the predicted risk; ×1.00 = no reaction).

  • Gradient boosting on summaries reacts to any dip, colds included: a drop moves the standard deviation and the 10th percentile whatever its shape. Since colds are frequent, this produces many false alarms.
  • Gradient boosting on raw days reacts only where it has seen the pattern before, mainly close to the prediction date. Each tree splits on single days, so a flare-up at day 30 and the same flare-up at day 130 are, for the model, unrelated events that must be learned separately from data.
  • The convolutional network reacts clearly to flare-ups at every position and much less to colds. Its filters are shared across all positions, so it learned the shape once — a drop followed by a slow, incomplete recovery — and recognises it anywhere. This is translation equivariance at work.
  • The LSTM also separates the two shapes, because its gated memory can carry "there was an incomplete recovery" forward. Its reaction, however, weakens with distance: strong for a flare-up 50 days before the prediction date, weak for one 150 days before. Gating slows the decay of memory; it does not abolish it. The CNN, which looks at all positions in parallel, does not have this recency bias.

The general lesson. Neural networks win when (i) the signal is a pattern in raw, high-dimensional input — a shape in a time series, a combination of words, an object in an image — that no small set of hand-made columns captures, and (ii) the architecture has the right inductive bias for that pattern: convolutions for local patterns anywhere, gated recurrence or attention (Lecture 2) for dependencies across long distances. In Practicals 1 and 2 the signal was already summarised in a few columns, and the advantage disappeared.

✏️ Exercise 3 — can an expert catch up by hand?¶

Knowing the mechanism, describe "dips followed by an incomplete recovery" with hand-made columns, add them to the summary features and refit gradient boosting. Proceed in two steps:

  1. a single column: the number of such dips in the window;
  2. four columns: the number, the day of the last such dip, the largest level lost after a dip, and the level shift (mean of the last 28 days minus mean of the first 90 days, relative to the person's level).

Does the gap to the CNN close? What does your answer say about the cost of feature engineering?

# ✅ SOLUTION
import warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore", category=RuntimeWarning)   # windows without a full 'after' period
def dip_features(X, dip=-0.7, lost=-0.12):
    """Hand-made description of 'a dip followed by an incomplete recovery'.
    dip:  3-day mean of centred log steps below -0.7 (about half the usual level)
    lost: mean level 8-20 days after the dip at least 0.12 (about 11%) below the 30 days before it"""
    worn = X > 0; L = np.log1p(X)
    level = (L * worn).sum(1, keepdims=True) / np.maximum(worn.sum(1, keepdims=True), 1)
    C = np.where(worn, L - level, np.nan)                                           # deviation from own level
    smooth = pd.DataFrame(C.T).rolling(3, min_periods=2).mean().values.T          # 3-day mean
    count, last_day, worst = np.zeros(len(X)), np.full(len(X), -1.0), np.zeros(len(X))
    for i in range(len(X)):
        last = -99
        for t in np.where(smooth[i] < dip)[0]:
            if t - last < 10 or t > W - 22: continue                               # one count per dip; need 20 days after it
            after, before = np.nanmean(C[i, t + 8:t + 20]), np.nanmean(C[i, max(0, t - 30):max(1, t - 3)])
            if after - before < lost:
                count[i] += 1; last_day[i] = t; worst[i] = min(worst[i], after - before)
            last = t
    shift = np.nanmean(C[:, -28:], 1) - np.nanmean(C[:, :90], 1)                   # recent level vs early level
    return np.c_[count, last_day, worst, shift]

Expected interpretation. A single count barely moves gradient boosting (average precision 0.07 in both cases), because it says neither when the dips happened nor how much level was lost. With four columns that describe timing, depth and the persistent shift, gradient boosting finds as many admissions in the top 5% as the CNN (47%), but its average precision (0.15) stays below the CNN and the LSTM (0.20): its ranking among the highest scores is less sharp. Two points matter more than the numbers. First, we could write these columns only because we know the mechanism: we wrote the generator. With real data, an analyst must guess thresholds (a 50% dip? an 11% loss?), window lengths and the definition of "incomplete", and every wrong guess loses information. Second, the networks learned the pattern without being told. Feature engineering works when the pattern is simple and understood; representation learning wins when it is subtle or unknown — and its discoveries can then inspire better hand-made features (Lecture 3 shows how to look inside a network).

7 · Would Arvena be allowed to use this?¶

  • Purpose. Using the score for a voluntary nurse call is care management. Using it to change the premium of an existing supplementary contract is not possible: Swiss supplementary tariffs apply to groups, and the insurer may not re-price or cancel a contract because the customer falls ill.
  • Consent and data protection. Step counts linked to health outcomes are health data, which the revised Federal Act on Data Protection (2023) treats as sensitive; their use requires explicit, informed consent for this purpose.
  • Fairness. People who walk little for reasons unrelated to health (a desk job, caring duties, a disability) must not be singled out; the model should be checked for such groups (Lecture 3).
